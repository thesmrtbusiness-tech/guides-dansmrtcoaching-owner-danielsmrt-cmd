Two people over 60 can eat about the same amount of food. One keeps strong legs. The other slowly gets weaker. Often, the difference isn't age. It's how much protein shows up at each meal.

Today I'm ranking five cheap, everyday proteins from worst to best for adults over 60. I ranked them on four things: protein per serving, cost, how easy they are to eat, and what to watch out for. Number one is soft, needs no cooking, and gets you most of the way to a full meal's worth of protein in a single bowl.

Before we start, here's how I ranked these, so you can check my thinking. Every food got judged the same four ways. How much protein is in a normal serving. How cheap it is. How easy it is to chew, prepare, and keep in your kitchen. And what you need to be careful about, like sodium or sugar. No brand is paying for this ranking, and the numbers come from standard nutrition charts. The studies and sources are listed in the description.

Now, why protein matters. After about age 30, we lose roughly 3 to 8 percent of our muscle each decade, and that loss speeds up later in life. That's called sarcopenia. Less muscle means weaker legs, worse balance, and slower walking.

Research groups that study aging suggest older adults do better with about 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight each day, which is more than the standard minimum. For a 150 pound person, that works out to roughly 68 to 82 grams a day.

And studies suggest that about 25 to 30 grams of protein per meal is the sweet spot for stimulating muscle. Under about 20 grams at a meal, the muscle response in older adults looks weaker.

So the goal isn't just more protein. It's enough protein at breakfast and lunch, not all saved for dinner. That's what this ranking is built around.

Quick note: amounts vary by brand, so check your labels. And if you have kidney disease or high blood pressure, or you take medication, talk to your doctor or dietitian before changing how much protein you eat. I'll remind you at the end.

Number five: peanut butter. It's cheap, tasty, and needs no prep. But a two tablespoon serving gives you only about 8 grams of protein, and it's loaded with calories, around 190. To reach 25 grams from peanut butter alone, you'd eat far too many calories.

The best way to use it is a spoonful on toast with milk or yogurt, as a helper, not your main protein. Pick one with no added sugar, and measure the spoonful at first. It's easy to pour more than you think.

Number four: canned beans. Very cheap, full of fiber, and filling. A half cup gives you about 7 to 9 grams of protein. That's good, but you'd need a big portion to hit 25 grams, and that can mean gas or bloating if you're not used to it.

The best way to use them is to mix beans with eggs or cheese, or add them to a soup that already has a protein in it. Rinse canned beans to cut the sodium. And if beans bother your stomach, start with a quarter cup and build up slowly.

Number three: eggs. About 6 grams each, cheap, soft, and quick to cook. Two eggs gets you about 12 grams. That's a solid start, but it's not the full 25 to 30 for a meal.

The best way to use them is two or three eggs, plus something else, like toast with peanut butter or a side of yogurt. Eat the whole egg. The yolk carries vitamins you lose with egg whites only. If your doctor has told you to watch your cholesterol, follow their advice.

Number two: canned tuna. A small can gives you roughly 17 to 22 grams of protein, with no cooking, a long shelf life, and some of the cheapest protein per dollar anywhere in the store. It almost won.

Two cautions. First, sodium. Choose low sodium cans, or drain and rinse. Second, mix up your fish, and ask your doctor how often is right for you, especially if you have high blood pressure or kidney problems.

The best way to use it is tuna mixed with plain Greek yogurt instead of heavy mayo, on whole grain toast or crackers.

And number one: plain Greek yogurt.

Here's why it wins. One cup gives you about 20 to 23 grams of protein, depending on the brand. That's the most protein per serving of anything on this list that needs no cooking and no chewing effort. It's soft. It's easy to find. It costs less per serving than most protein snacks. And it pairs well with everything else we ranked.

Here's how to make it a full meal: one cup of plain Greek yogurt, plus a hard boiled egg or a spoonful of peanut butter, plus berries. That lands you right around 25 to 30 grams.

Pick plain, not flavored, to avoid a pile of added sugar. Cottage cheese is a very close runner up, with about 12 to 15 grams per half cup, and it works just as well if you prefer it.

Now here's one more way to look at this list, because it shows something the ranking doesn't. Let's compare how much protein you get for every 100 calories. Canned tuna comes out on top, with about 20 grams. Greek yogurt is close behind, around 18. Eggs give you about 8. Beans, about 6 or 7. And peanut butter is last, at around 4 grams.

So if calories are your concern, tuna actually beats yogurt. Yogurt still wins overall because it has no sodium problem, needs no opener or fork, and works at any meal. That's why a ranking needs more than one number.

Here's what a full day could look like, using this list. Breakfast: a cup of plain Greek yogurt with berries and one boiled egg. That's close to 30 grams. Lunch: a small can of tuna mixed with a little yogurt, on whole grain toast. That's another 25 to 30. Then whatever protein you normally eat for dinner, and you're right in the range researchers suggest, without any powders or supplements.

If chewing is hard for you, notice that the three softest foods on this list, yogurt, eggs, and tuna, are also the ones that carry the most protein per serving. That's not an accident.

Now, three mistakes that cancel out the benefits. Mistake one: eating almost no protein at breakfast, then catching up at dinner. Your muscles respond better to protein spread across the day. Mistake two: buying flavored yogurt, which can carry as much sugar as a dessert. Mistake three: relying only on peanut butter and beans, because they're cheap, and never reaching 25 grams at a meal.

Here's a simple plan for this week. At breakfast, build a plate around number one or number three. At lunch, use number two with yogurt instead of mayo. Use beans and peanut butter as helpers, not heroes.

And pair protein with some walking or leg exercise, when your doctor says it's okay. Protein feeds muscle, but movement tells muscle to use it.

One more time, because it matters: if you have kidney disease or high blood pressure, or you're on medication, ask your doctor or a dietitian how much protein and sodium is right for you.
